Spanish pronunciation in plain English

How do I say it?

This guide uses contemporary Peninsular Spanish as the experiment’s reference variety while noting major Latin-American differences where they matter. It covers the sounds most likely to appear in Poco a Poco without assuming knowledge of phonetic symbols.

5

Begin with five steady vowel sounds

Give each vowel one clean sound. Do not let it wander through several sounds as English vowels often do.

The vowels

A, E and I

A like the broad a in “father”. E like a clear “eh”. I like “ee” in “machine”.

rataRAH-tah
elefanteeh-leh-FAHN-teh
sísee

O and U

O is a clean “oh”. U is like “oo” in “rule”. Keep both short and stable.

burroBOO-rroh
mulaMOO-lah

Vowels together

Spanish often joins neighbouring vowels smoothly. Read them clearly before trying to speed up.

cuálkwahl
pequeñopeh-KEH-nyoh

Written accents

An accent marks the stressed syllable or distinguishes one word from another.

quékeh
sísee

The consonants that surprise English speakers

C and Z

In much of Spain, c before e or i and z sound like th in “thin”. In most of Latin America they sound like s. Both are standard.

cincoTHEEN-koh / SEEN-koh

J and soft G

Use a strong breathy sound, similar to the sound in Scottish or Irish “loch”.

jirafahee-RAH-fah
genteHEN-teh

LL and Y

In most modern varieties these are pronounced alike, usually near English y in “yes”, although regional forms vary.

llamaYAH-mah
yoyoh

R and RR

A single r is a brief tap. Initial r and written rr are stronger trills. Do not delay speaking while trying to perfect the trill.

rataRAH-tah
burroBOO-rroh

Ñ

Ñ sounds like the middle of “canyon”.

pequeñopeh-KEH-nyoh

H and B/V

H is silent. Spanish b and v are normally pronounced alike, with the exact sound depending on position.

hablaAH-blah
vacaBAH-kah

Stress without panic

Words ending in a vowel, n or s

Normally stress the second-to-last syllable: eleFANte, aniMAles.

Most other endings

Normally stress the final syllable: aniMAL. A written accent overrides the usual rule.

A practical standard, not a reason to freeze: Spanish varies across countries and regions. Clear vowels and consistency matter more than an exaggerated imitation of any accent.

A 30-second practice routine

  1. Say the word slowly and give every vowel a clean sound.
  2. Check whether c, g, j, ll, ñ or r changes the sound.
  3. Say the whole word again naturally.
  4. Make one recording after study and compare clarity, not theatricality.
