A plain-English sound guide

Spanish pronunciation.

The same supporting role as the Ecclesiastical Latin pronunciation page, adapted for the Spanish used in Poco a Poco. Start with clear, consistent sounds rather than chasing a perfect accent.

Working convention: the experiment will use contemporary Peninsular Spanish as its reference variety, while noting major Latin-American differences where they matter. The book itself is older and does not prescribe one modern recording standard.

a, e, i, o, u

Spanish vowels are generally short and stable. Avoid turning them into the gliding vowel sounds common in English.

Examples: rata, elefante, mula, pequeño, burro

j and soft g

A strong breathy sound, rather like a firmer Scottish or Irish pronunciation of the sound in “loch”.

Examples: jirafa; gente; gigante

ll and y

In most modern varieties these are pronounced alike, usually near the English y in “yes”, though regional forms vary.

Examples: llama; yo; mayor

r and rr

A single r is a brief tap. Initial r and written rr are stronger trills. Do not delay speaking while trying to perfect the trill.

Examples: rata; perro; burro

c and z

In much of Spain, c before e or i and z use the sound in English “thin”. In most of Latin America they use s. Both are standard.

Examples: cinco; hace; pequeño

Stress and accents

Words ending in a vowel, n or s normally stress the penultimate syllable; most others stress the final syllable. A written accent marks an exception or distinguishes words.

Examples: elefante; animal; sí; qué; cuáles
Lesson I practice: read the questions slowly once, then again as connected speech: ¿Qué es el elefante? Es un animal. ¿Es grande la rata? No, señor.
