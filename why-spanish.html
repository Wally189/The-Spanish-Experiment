The question behind the experiment

Why Spanish?

Because a language is rarely just vocabulary and grammar. It is a place, a people, a memory, and sometimes a life that has not happened yet.

Most language-learning stories begin with a sensible plan. Mine did not. I learnt French at school, which gave me my first glimpse of the peculiar delight of another language: familiar ideas arranged differently, sounded differently and sometimes even felt differently. Spanish arrived later, not through a timetable, but through Spain itself.

After many visits over the years, I fell in love with the country: its cities and villages, its landscapes, its food, its pace of life and the extraordinary variety contained within a single nation. Spain is not one thing. Asturias is not Andalucía; Galicia is not Madrid; the Atlantic north can feel like another world from the Mediterranean south.

For years I carried a rather specific dream: to live and work in northern Spain, somewhere around Asturias or Galicia, before eventually retiring further south. Life intervened. The dream was postponed, not abandoned.

A language with a large world behind it

Spanish is not merely useful for holidays. It is one of Europe’s major languages and one of the great international languages of the modern world. It connects Spain with much of Latin America and with communities far beyond both.

That scale matters. Learning Spanish opens the door not to one culture, but to many: different histories, accents, traditions, literatures, cuisines and ways of seeing the world. A language becomes more than a private hobby when it allows you to speak to millions of people you could not otherwise properly know.

A Romance language — and a living one

Spanish belongs to the Romance family, alongside French, Italian, Portuguese and others, all shaped by Latin. I am learning Latin too, and the relationship between the two fascinates me. Words change, grammar shifts and sounds wander off in unexpected directions, yet traces of the older language remain everywhere.

Latin is not simply a fossil behind glass. In Spanish, part of its afterlife is still being spoken every day. But Spanish is also emphatically alive: a language of homes, streets, churches, workplaces, jokes, arguments and ordinary friendship.

The reason closest to home

I have many Spanish-speaking friends through my church. They are perfectly capable of speaking to me in English, but that is not quite the point. Friendship should not always require the other person to cross the linguistic bridge. I would like to meet them halfway.

I want to understand more of their humour, their expressions and the things that do not survive translation intact. I want to be able to speak naturally rather than assemble a sentence in my head and abandon it halfway through.

I also want to attend Mass in Spanish and follow it properly: not merely recognise the familiar shape of the liturgy, but understand the readings, prayers and homily as they are spoken. Faith is universal, but it is always encountered through particular languages, voices and communities.

The problem was never beginning

I have dabbled in Spanish for several years. My comprehension is often better than I expect, particularly when reading. I can recognise a surprising amount and usually grasp the general sense. Yet I have repeatedly fallen into the same trap: starting, stopping, changing resources, changing methods and beginning again.

This creates the illusion of progress. Every new course feels promising because the opening lessons are familiar. Greetings, numbers, colours and basic verbs all return. The real difficulty comes later, when novelty fades and structure becomes necessary.

The Spanish Experiment is my attempt to stop flipping. Instead of endlessly searching for the perfect course, I am building a personalised and structured path through one.

The aim is not to present myself as an expert. It is to document the process of becoming less inexpert: what works, what fails, what I misunderstand and what finally begins to stick.

Why your own “why” matters

Language learning demands time, repetition and a willingness to sound slightly foolish. That becomes much easier when the reason for learning is stronger than the inconvenience.

Perhaps your reason is family. Perhaps it is travel, work, faith, literature, music, heritage or the hope of living somewhere new. Perhaps you simply like the sound of the language and want to understand the world carried inside it.

There is no single correct reason. But it helps to identify one that belongs to you. “I ought to learn Spanish” is easy to postpone. A specific desire — speaking to a friend, understanding a grandparent, attending Mass, reading a favourite author or living in another country — has far more power.

My reasons are Spain, friendship, faith, curiosity and an old dream that has not entirely gone away. Yours may be completely different.

Find your reason

This website follows my attempt to learn Spanish steadily, honestly and poco a poco. You are welcome to follow along, use the materials and adapt the method to your own life. Before beginning, answer one question: Why do I want to learn Spanish? The grammar will tell you how to continue. Your reason will tell you why.
